Who he is

The scientist who explains the why.

Andrew Huberman is a professor of neurobiology and ophthalmology at Stanford School of Medicine. On Huberman Lab he talks through research on sleep, morning light, exercise timing, cold exposure and stress, and turns it into routines people can try.

What sets his work apart from most health content is that he explains why a routine might help, which makes it easier to stick with. Morning light is a good example: Huberman says it helps set the body's daily rhythm, and he links that rhythm to how you sleep and how sharp you feel the next day.

The core insight

What you take in shapes what you can do.

You can't out-discipline a broken sleep schedule.
Our summary of a theme from Huberman Lab, not a direct quote

MAN borrows a way of thinking from Huberman rather than a protocol. Huberman says that sleep, light, movement and what you consume set the conditions for everything else you try to do. That fits what MAN sees: the best identity work gets harder when you're short on sleep and running on scraps.

That's why Nourishment is a full pillar in MAN, and why, when it's steady, Mindset and Action take less effort.

How MAN uses it

The Nourishment pillar.

The N in MAN draws on the topics Huberman covers: sleep, morning light, movement, and what you eat, watch, listen to and give your attention to. MAN treats them as things you can notice and adjust one at a time, and leaves the medical detail to your GP.

The Nourishment guide turns this into one small daily rep, such as a glass of water before your first coffee, and a short end-of-day note on what you took in. The question behind both is simple: does this help the man you're becoming, or make tomorrow harder for him?

Inputs you can change

Sleep, light, movement and what you consume can all be adjusted, even though most of us treat them as background.

Steady makes it easier

When what you take in is steady, the daily rep in the other two pillars takes less effort.

Consistency over intensity

Huberman often makes the point that regular, moderate habits do more than occasional big efforts, so a steady bedtime beats one perfect night and a daily walk beats a weekly hard session.
