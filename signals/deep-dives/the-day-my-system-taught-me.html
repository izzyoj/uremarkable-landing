The Day My System Taught Me

Six principles encoded in one day. None of them by reading.

Baz Ozturk · ~2,800 words · 12 min read
The Wall

7am.

Quota gone.

Two Instagram reels sitting in my workspace queue. Both unread. One from Catherine Wu — her breakdown of Claude setup architecture. One from Scott — his model on why insight doesn't equal behaviour change.

Couldn't touch either. The Gemini free tier had hit its ceiling overnight.

Default move: flag it, wait for reset, come back tomorrow.

Default moves are what got me here.

So instead: spawn a mission task to the research agent. Groq Whisper. Pull the transcripts directly. Results back in four minutes.

Work continued.

But the transcript wasn't what stayed with me.

The gap was.

Why didn't that routing happen automatically?

I'd built a system. CLAUDE.md. Twenty skills with documented triggers. Scheduled rituals. Precall guides for every product. A five-agent architecture that runs while I sleep. And when it hit the most basic block imaginable — API quota exhausted — it stopped cold and waited.

That gap was the story of the whole day.

Scott's Model (And Its Timing Was Not Accidental)

When the Groq transcript finally came through, the insight that had been sitting behind the quota wall landed.

Scott's model. The actual mechanism behind why insight doesn't produce change.

The brain runs on predictions. Stimulus X leads to outcome Y — learned over years of evidence. Old behaviours have thousands of repetitions backing them. New ones have zero. When a breakthrough idea lands — a book, a video, a moment of sudden clarity — there's a dopamine spike.

That spike feels like change.

It isn't.

No action means no prediction error. No prediction error means no model update. No model update means the old behaviour runs unchanged.

The actual chain is this:

The Neural Plasticity Formula
Information ↓ Action ↓ Feedback ↓ Adjustment ↓ Neural Plasticity You change at step 5. Not step 1.

You don't change when you understand something. You change when you act on it and the environment responds differently than the old model predicted. Insight is just the first step. Without the rest of the chain, it's a feeling of change dressed up as the real thing.

Get yourself together man ...

Because I'd just built a stack of documented principles, assumed I was building a better system, and then hit the first basic block — and the system hadn't updated its behaviour at all.

I understood the principle.

The system didn't act on it.

Scott was right.

The Gap the Block Revealed

The routing-around worked. Groq came back clean. Transcripts processed. Day continued. But the thing that actually mattered was the question the block forced:

Why did I have to think of this? Why wasn't it already there?

CLAUDE.md had rules for things. Rituals for things. Tone for things. Skills with explicit triggers. But it had no trained response to the pattern "blocked — find another path."

The absence wasn't about knowledge. The information was sitting in the system. I knew the research agent could pull transcripts. I knew Groq had a Whisper endpoint.

But information without a triggered response pathway is just storage.

Think about how a ship's navigator operated before GPS. No satellites. No digital chart. Just the open ocean and instruments that failed in storms. What kept them on course wasn't a rulebook covering every weather condition. It was dead reckoning — the principle that position can always be calculated from speed, heading, and time elapsed. When the fog rolled in, when the coast disappeared, when instruments gave out — the schema fired. Not because they'd enumerated every sea state. Because they'd encoded the underlying geometry of movement.

Rules: → "if fog, do X" Schema: → "if you can't see the destination, here's how you calculate where you are"

The gap in CLAUDE.md wasn't a knowledge gap.

It was a schema gap.

Mark Kashef's Argument (He Was Right About the Wrong Thing)

I was mid-draft of the fix when Mark Kashef's counterpoint surfaced — and it arrived at exactly the right moment to make the answer sharper.

His position: CLAUDE.md files that grow by adding a rule for every failure are expensive and brittle. Every new edge case adds a new rule. The file bloats. Token cost per conversation rises. Generalisation drops.

He's right about the failure mode.

The instinct after a near-miss is always: write a rule for this specific thing. If X happens, do Y. It feels like progress. It is — for that exact scenario.

But directive lists are lookup tables:

The Lookup Table Problem
DIRECTIVE LIST:
- if quota_exhausted: wait for reset
- if tool_unavailable: flag and stop
- if permission_denied: ask user
- if rate_limited: retry after delay
- if api_deprecated: ???
- if [situation you haven't seen yet]: ???

Pattern matches and fires when the exact condition is met. Miss the condition by one variable — the rule doesn't trigger. New edge case — new rule. New rule — more bloat. More bloat — higher token cost, lower generalisation.

You can't enumerate every block type in advance. Trying to means the system only handles situations you've already seen.

But the counter-argument is sharper than the critique. Here's the eight-line reasoning block that replaced forty potential rules:

The Reasoning Block
When you hit a hard stop mid-task, the failure isn't
the block itself — it's invisible silence.

Challenge yourself: is there an alternative path?
A mission task? A fallback API? A different tool?

If yes, take it. If no, hand off cleanly with context.

Never wait silently. A clean handoff beats a quiet failure.

That eight lines covers: quota exhausted, missing credentials, permission denied, tool unavailable, service unreachable, rate limited, API deprecated — and every permutation you haven't seen yet. Not by listing them. By encoding the geometry of the problem.

The Distinction That Changes Everything
DIRECTIVE LIST REASONING BLOCK ───────────────── ───────────────────── if X → do Y understand the principle if A → do B derive the response if C → do D in any situation if [new] → ??? including ones not seen yet Lookup table. Schema. Pattern match. First principles. Breaks at the edge. Generalises.

Kashef is right that bloated directive lists are a failure mode. He's wrong about the solution. The answer isn't shorter CLAUDE.md files. It's encoding reasoning instead of rules. Fewer words. More power.

A lookup table retrieves.

A schema generalises.

One schema covers ten situations you never wrote rules for.

The Copy That Was Sitting in the Wrong Quadrant

Afternoon. FounderBud hero copy review.

Strong Pull. No Push.

"FounderBud lives in Telegram. Text it, voice it, send a video. Does your work."

Features front and centre. Product leading. Problem nowhere. Then the Telegram objection surfaced: WhatsApp is common. Snapchat, SMS, Teams — all common. Telegram isn't. Before a founder even registers what FounderBud does, they've already hit a friction wall.

That's not a Pull problem. That's Habits and Anxiety firing before Push has had a chance to land.

JTBD market forces. Four of them. They govern every adoption decision in every market, every time:

The Four Forces of Switching
PUSH PULL (frustration with now) (attraction to new) ↓ ↑ ┌──────────────────────────┐ │ SWITCHING HAPPENS │ │ when top > bottom │ └──────────────────────────┘ ↑ ↓ HABITS ANXIETY (comfort of current) (risk of changing)

Push + Pull must outweigh Anxiety + Habits for a switch to happen. Most product copy lives entirely in Pull — features, benefits, proof points. But Pull only converts when Push has already fired. If someone isn't feeling the cost of staying where they are, more Pull is just noise.

The Push for FounderBud: founders are already paying for Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity. None of them remember the previous session. None of them stack. Every project starts from scratch. Work builds up in chat histories that are never found again.

That's the felt cost. That's the Push.

So the hero flipped:

"You've paid for Claude, ChatGPT, and Perplexity. None of them remember you." FounderBud hero — post-JTBD audit

Two sentences. The problem named before the product. Push before Pull.

Then the same lens went across every section of the page. Six sections audited. Each one checked: which quadrant is this copy sitting in? Pull before Push — wrong quadrant. Rewrite.

Demo section → rewritten from feature to felt outcome Ships bar → flipped from capability list to cost-of-waiting Pricing section → reframed around ownership, not access

One framework. Whole page changed.

The fraud analyst instinct from Gumtree days: always check the primary source, not the derived signal. The Vernon Partners cards would've gone out green if I'd trusted the hero image instead of the website. Same principle. Different domain.

The Marketplace Nobody Had Named Yet

Somewhere in the FounderBud copy work, a new concept surfaced that I hadn't seen coming.

SaaS is struggling because interoperability kills moats. Notion won't talk to Monday. Monday won't talk to Airtable. Platforms survive by locking users in — refusing to share context. But FounderBud's outputs aren't trapped in a chat window. They're files on your machine. And the "asks" that produce those outputs — the tested, previewed, domain-specific templates — aren't just prompts.

A prompt → just text. Untested. Unknown output. An ask → tuned interaction pattern. Tested. Preview before you run it.
The Ask as Unit of Value
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ COLD OUTREACH ASK │ │ │ │ Domain: Sales / Founder Outreach │ │ Tested: 50+ real scenarios │ │ Preview: [see output format first] │ │ │ │ Output → qualified lead entry │ │ + personalised email │ │ + follow-up sequence │ │ │ │ Run it? [Yes] [Preview] │ └─────────────────────────────────────────┘

You don't have to trust the description. You see what you get before you run it. That's the proof layer. That's what makes it defensible: not the content, but the curation plus evidence.

A marketplace of asks across life, business, family, health — any domain. Each one with a preview. Each one someone else can pull off the shelf without a setup call.

That's how the service scales beyond one person's implementation bandwidth.

Strategy doc saved. Decision register updated. Platform direction now on the record with reasoning — so future sessions don't re-debate the same ground.

The Manifesto That Closed the Loop

The vault-first manifesto came last. And it came because someone needed to write down what all of this was actually for.

MAN. JobBud. FounderBud. StudyBestie. AIgileBud. SalesBud.

Six products. Every one of them hired for a different job. Every one of them built from two principles:

Vault-first. If a human owns their outcome, they own their data. Not rented access to it — the thing itself, on their machine, accumulating, theirs forever. The opposite of SaaS. The opposite of a platform that resets every session and holds your history hostage to a subscription.

Why-led. Every product starts with the felt cost of staying where you are, not with the features of the solution. JTBD isn't a marketing framework in this stack. It's the design principle. The question before anything is built: what job is this human hired for? What would it cost them to stay where they are?

The thread connecting every product isn't the tech, the price, or the use case. It's a consistent bet: people are better served by tools that remember them than by platforms that rent them access to their own data.

That bet is now in writing.

It lives in the vault.

It's the answer to "what is all of this for?"

What You Can Do With This

Three things. Concrete. Implementable today.

Takeaway 01
Check whether you're adding rules or reasoning

Next time you're tempted to add an instruction to your system prompt — ask: is this a directive (if X do Y) or a principle (here's the geometry of the problem)? Directives break at the edge. Principles generalise. Before you write it, convert the directive into a reasoning block. The test: would this still apply to a situation you haven't seen yet?

Takeaway 02
Audit your copy for quadrant

Open your landing page, pitch deck, or email sequence. Check every section: which JTBD force is it speaking to? If Push hasn't fired and you're already in Pull — you're in the wrong quadrant. Name the felt cost of staying where they are before you name the feature. Pull only lands after Push has done its job.

Takeaway 03
Treat every block as a schema request

When your system hits a wall and stops — that's not a failure to document with a new rule. That's the system asking: where's my reasoning block for this? Write the principle, not the rule. One good reasoning block covers ten situations you haven't seen yet. That's the compounding you're actually after.

The question is never whether your system fails.

The question is whether failure leaves a trace.
